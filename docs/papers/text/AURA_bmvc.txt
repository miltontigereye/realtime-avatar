                  cache, requiring only the audio of the current chunk rather than the multi-second au-

      with the teacher, by training it to match the teacher’s trajectories with fewer diffusion
      steps and a chunk-wise causal attention mask, then applies Distribution Matching Distil-

1    Introduction
Recent speech-to-speech models [12, 37] are able to model full-duplex voice interaction in
real-time, paving the way for voice assistants that hold natural and smooth conversations.
However, these models only generate audio signal. In contrast, human spoken communi-
cation relies on visual cues and facial expressions, via an interplay of the audio and visual
modalities. For instance, lip motion alters perceived phonemes [33], visual cues sharpen
comprehension in noise [43], and an embodied talking face enhances the sense of social pres-
ence over voice alone [6]. To model such behaviors, audio-driven video generation models
can synthesize talking-head videos from a reference image and an input speech audio, en-
abling applications such as virtual avatars, synthetic presenters, and non-player characters
in games. All these applications, however, share a common constraint: the avatar must be
generated on-the-fly, as the audio arrives, from a stream of audio of unbounded duration.
    State-of-the-art talking-head models such as TalkVerse [47] and Wan-S2V [16] produce
high-quality audio-driven videos but are ill-suited to the streaming setting. Because they rely
on bidirectional attention, they must generate entire video chunks of several seconds at once,
running dozens of denoising steps over the full chunk before producing any frame, which on
a single GPU takes minutes (per chunk). Recently, CausVid [53] and Self-Forcing [22] have
explored distilling such bidirectional video diffusion teachers into few-step causal students
that generate video chunk by chunk in an autoregressive manner. However, these methods
target only the text-to-video and image-to-video settings. Extending this approach to the
audio-driven setting, where the model must additionally track a dense time-aligned speech
signal, has so far been demonstrated only on much larger models (14–18B parameters) [23,
31, 38], which remain too large for real-time inference on a single GPU (only 2 fps). A
second obstacle is the scarcity of suitable training data: existing public talking-head datasets
such as Hallo3 [9] and CelebV [57] consist of clips too short to cover the chunk length of
modern teachers like TalkVerse, ruling them out for distillation (see Section 3.6).
    We introduce AURA, a 5B-parameter causal few-step speech-to-video (S2V) model that
generates streaming talking-head videos from live audio at 720p resolution and 9.7 fps on
a single GPU, a 17× speedup over its teacher. For training, we generate and release a
new talking-head dataset comprising 30K videos (38.7 hours) produced with the TalkVerse
teacher, conditioned on reference images and text descriptions from the Hallo3 dataset [9]
and synthetic speech audio. To train our model, we use a two-stage distillation pipeline,
similar to CausVid: Stage 1 first trains the causal student to match the bidirectional teacher
(TalkVerse [47])’s Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) trajectories in order to reduce the
gap between the bidirectional teacher and the causal student architecture. Then, Stage 2
closes the few-step gap with Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD) and backward sim-
ulation: by training the student on intermediate states from its own denoising rollouts, the
COHEN ET AL.: AURA: AUDIO-DRIVEN STREAMING AVATAR                                           3

Stage 2 loss reflects accumulating errors the student may actually produce at inference.
    To further improve efficiency, we train the model to support chunk-wise causal attention,
allowing us to use a rolling KV cache with bounded memory at inference. We also use a
single KV cache for all diffusion denoising timesteps, further reducing memory footprint.
    With this approach, on standard talking-avatar benchmarks, AURA matches its bidirec-
tional teacher on visual quality (FVD [45] and JEDi [32] metrics) and audio-visual align-
ment, while running 17× faster with only four denoising steps per chunk. AURA delivers
720p streaming video at 9.7 fps on a single GPU, faster than all baselines we compare against
in the same single-GPU setting. Figure 1 shows an excerpt of qualitative samples generated
by AURA, as well as its speed–quality tradeoff against prior work.
    In summary, we introduce AURA, which makes the following contributions:
• A 5B causal few-step S2V model that matches its bidirectional teacher on FVD and JEDi
  metrics, with a 17× speedup. Our inference pipeline streams 720p video at 9.7 fps on a
  single GPU, the highest throughput among audio-driven baselines we compare against.
  Our method is based on a two-stage distillation method (ODE-trajectory initialization
  followed by Distribution Matching Distillation with backward simulation) paired with a
  streaming inference scheme based on a rolling KV cache of past chunks;
• A public dataset of 30K teacher-generated talking-head ODE trajectories paired with syn-
  thetic speech, released to support future research on streaming S2V distillation;


2    Related Work
Diffusion and Flow Matching. Image Generative models have evolved through several
paradigms: Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) [4, 17, 24] are efficient as they gener-
ate images in a single forward pass but suffer from known limitations around mode coverage
and training stability. More recently, diffusion models [14, 19, 25, 36, 40, 41] and flow
matching [28, 30] have become the dominant paradigm for image generation. Indeed, both
frameworks are closely related and model data through an iterative multi-step refinement
process, yielding stable training with high sample diversity but at the cost of slow sampling.
Most modern large-scale video generators, including our teacher TalkVerse [47], are trained
under the flow matching framework, and our work also builds on this formulation.
Video Diffusion Models. Recent work has further scaled diffusion for video generation.
Early efforts such as Imagen Video [20], Make-A-Video [39], Align Your Latents [3], and
Stable Video Diffusion [2] extended image diffusion to short clips, typically by inflating 2D
architectures with temporal layers. More recent open-source and industrial models, Sora [5],
Movie Gen [34], Open-Sora [56], CogVideoX [50], HunyuanVideo [26], and Wan [46]—
adopt diffusion transformer (DiT) architectures and operate in a compressed latent space,
scaling to 720p–1080p resolutions and minute-long clips. These models are typically non-
causal: they generate the entire video sequence jointly over 50 or more denoising steps, and
are thus not well adapted to the latency requirements of streaming applications. Current
SotA talking-head models are based on such architectures. For instance, Hallo3 [9] adapts
CogVideoX [50] for portrait animation with a DiT-adapted identity reference network, while
closed-source systems such as OmniHuman [27] extend DiT-based audio-driven generation
to full-body animation. In this work, we use TalkVerse [47], which builds on the Wan2.2-
5B architecture, as a non-causal multi-step distillation teacher and distill it into a causal,
few-step student suitable for streaming generation.
4                             COHEN ET AL.: AURA: AUDIO-DRIVEN STREAMING AVATAR

Distillation of Diffusion Models. The slow inference of diffusion models has motivated
extensive work on distillation, training a faster few-step student to reproduce a slow teacher’s
output distribution. One particularly successful line is Distribution Matching Distillation
(DMD) [52], which trains a one-step student to minimize the reverse KL to the teacher via
a learned critic; DMD2 [51] extends it to a few-step generator and introduces backward
simulation, computing the loss on the student’s own rollouts rather than forward-diffused
data. CausVid [53] applies DMD to distill a non-causal video teacher into a causal few-step
student for streaming text-to-video. A challenge specific to causal video distillation is the
train/test gap: the student is trained on teacher-provided past frames but must condition on its
own previously generated frames at inference. Self-Forcing [22] and its variants [10, 29, 49,
58] address this temporal gap by rolling the student out autoregressively through a training-
time KV cache with stop-gradient. We instead build on DMD2’s backward simulation [51],
which exposes the student to its own denoising trajectory, and adapt it to streaming video
(Sec. 3.4) so that the student also conditions on its own past generations, without maintaining
a training-time cache.
Streaming Talking Heads. A handful of recent works tackle audio-driven talking-head
generation in a streaming and few steps setting. Talking Machines [31] is, to our knowledge,
the first to address this regime, distilling a 18B model with DMD and sparse causal attention;
weights are not released. Live Avatar [23] distills the 14B Wan-S2V model [16] into a 14B
few-step student that runs at 16 fps and 480p on a 5-GPU setup. SoulX-FlashTalk [38] adopts
a similar few-step distillation strategy from a 14B teacher into a 14B student and requires 4
GPUs to reach real-time throughput. In contrast, AURA distills a 5B teacher into a same-size
causal 4-step student that delivers 720p video at 9.7 fps on a single GPU. Closest to our work
is Avatar Forcing [11] whose pre-print was concurrently published: It uses a similar DMD-
based distillation process but requires a look-ahead window of future audio to generate each
frame by design, leading to a small yet incompressible streaming delay.


3     Method
In this section, we present AURA, our two-stage pipeline that distills a non-causal audio-
driven video diffusion teacher (TalkVerse) into a 4-step causal student, streaming 720p
avatars in constant memory. We first recall Distribution Matching Distillation (Sec. 3.1)
and the TalkVerse teacher (Sec. 3.1) to set out notation and context. Section 3.2 then intro-
duces the causal student and the per-chunk mixed-timestep design that enables a compact
KV cache. Section 3.3 and Section 3.4 cover the two training stages, and Section 3.5 finally
describes streaming inference.


3.1    Preliminaries
Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD). DMD [52] trains a few-step student gener-
ator Gθ to match a multi-step teacher diffusion model at the distribution level, rather than
along individual sampling trajectories. Concretely, DMD minimizes, in expectation over the
diffusion timestep t ∼ U (0, 1), the reverse KL divergence between the student distribution
pgen and the data distribution pdata , both diffused to noise level t. We adopt t ∈ [0, 1] through-
out this paper, with t = 0 corresponding to clean latents and t = 1 to pure noise. While the
densities pgen and pdata are intractable, the gradient of the KL with respect to θ admits a

Backward simulation. Directly applying DMD to multi-step students yields a training-
inference mismatch: at training time, the student is fed forward-diffused real samples xt =
Ψ(x0 ,t), whereas at inference time, intermediate inputs come from previous denoising steps
of the student itself. To close this gap, DMD2 [51] introduces backward simulation. At each
training iteration, the student is unrolled for a few denoising steps to produce a synthetic
intermediate sample x̂t , which then replaces the forward-diffused real sample as input to
the DMD objective. Because Gθ is itself a few-step generator, this unrolling is cheap. The
DMD gradient and the score-matching update for sgen,η are then computed at x̂t rather than
at Ψ(x0 ,t), aligning the input distribution seen during training with the one produced at
inference. We also adopt this scheme in the second state of our training pipeline (Sec. 3.4).
TalkVerse Architecture. We use TalkVerse [47] as our teacher model, a 5B-parameter
audio-driven video diffusion model built on the Wan2.2-5B [46] DiT backbone; Its video
VAE uses compression factors (t, h, w) = (4, 16, 16) that enable efficient 720p generation.
The model is conditioned on a reference identity image, a speech audio track encoded with
Wav2Vec2 [1] and injected via cross-attention to temporally aligned visual tokens, and a text
prompt encoded with T5-XXL [35]. Long videos are generated with a sliding-window mech-
anism that conditions each window on a set of motion frames compressed by a FramePack
module [55], and the reference image is assigned a positional offset placed ahead of the de-
noised window. Inference uses 50 denoising steps with a flow-matching solver and classifier-
free guidance. We provide a more detailed description in the supplementary material A.1.

3.2    AURA Architecture
To turn the 50-step bidirectional teacher (TalkVerse) into a 4-step causal student, we intro-
duce three architectural modifications: chunked causal attention, removal of motion frames,
and per-chunk mixed timesteps. In combination, these changes also enable us to keep a
compact rolling KV cache as illustrated in Figure 2b.
Chunked causal attention. The student generates videos in chunks of nc = 4 latent frames
(∼ 0.64 s at the 25 Hz native rate), whereas the teacher generates 30 latent frames (∼ 5 s) in
a single pass. We enforce causality between chunks in the student: during training, a block-
wise causal attention mask prevents frames in chunk Ci from attending to frames in chunks
C j for j > i, while attention is bidirectional within a chunk (Figure 2a).
No motion frames. TalkVerse normally conditions each generated video sequence on a
window of past frames (motion frames) to maintain temporal coherence across windows. In
our causal student, the KV cache already carries the past context required for coherence,
thus we remove the motion frame mechanism entirely. This also saves us the cost of a few
forward passes through the model required to process motion frames for every new chunk.

Per-chunk mixed timesteps: a compact KV cache. At 720p, the KV cache is already large
at a single noise level due to the high spatial token count per frame. A naive implementation
that would scale one (temporal) KV cache per diffusion step would increase the memory
cost by the number of denoising steps per chunk. To avoid this, we train the model to handle
sequences in which different chunks are at different noise levels: each chunk is assigned
a single diffusion timestep ti , shared by all frames within the chunk (Fig. 3, center). This
per-chunk mixed timesteps scheme follows CausVid [53], which adopts it without explicit
motivation; we argue it is a key design choice to enable a compact streaming KV cache.
At inference, the current chunk is fed at a high noise level tcur while previously generated
chunks, cached in the KV cache, are at the lowest noise level reached during their own
generation. The cache therefore stores keys and values at a single low noise level per past
chunk, rather than at every diffusion step (Fig. 2b). In contrast, prior streaming audio-driven
models that retain multiple noise levels in the cache pay a proportional memory cost [23].

causal student to follow the teacher ODE under the new architecture (Fig. 3, top), bringing
the student close enough to the teacher distribution so that Stage 2 can converge.
Training data. We train Stage 1 on our synthetic dataset described in Sec. 3.6. Each video
comes with latents stored at five timesteps along a 50-step ODE trajectory from the teacher.
At training time, the student is queried at the four non-zero timesteps {0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1} and

3.4     Stage 2: Backward Simulation DMD
Stage 1 yields a causal few-step student that closely follows teacher ODE trajectories, but its
sample quality remains bounded by the compression from many-step to few-step inference.

student output distribution toward the teacher distribution, with a sampling strategy adapted
to our causal, chunked setting (Fig. 3, bottom).

role of sgen,η in Eq. 1. Following the two time-scale update rule of DMD2 [51], the critic is

Backward simulation. A central question in few-step distillation is which samples x̂0 to

proceed in three steps: (i) we run a full multi-step rollout of Gθ without backpropagation to
obtain the intermediate noisy states {x̂tk } (tk ∈ {0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1}) along its own sampling
trajectory; (ii) we re-feed these states through Gθ with gradients enabled, using per-chunk
mixed timesteps (Sec. 3.2) sampled uniformly across the trajectory; (iii) we evaluate the
DMD gradient (Eq. 3) at the resulting x̂0 (Fig. 3, bottom). The student is thus supervised at
the intermediate states it encounters at inference, reducing the training–inference mismatch.

3.5    Inference: Causal Generation with rolling KV Cache
At inference, the student generates the video autoregressively, one chunk of 4 latent frames at
a time, in 4 diffusion steps per chunk. Within each chunk, we adopt the predict-and-renoise
scheme of DMD2 [51] (see supplementary material A.2). To extend generation to longer se-
quences, we use a rolling KV cache with a first-chunk anchor that provides temporal context
across chunks, and a RoPE [42] re-indexing scheme that bridges training and inference.
Rolling KV cache. The sampling scheme of DMD2 describes the denoising of a single
chunk. To generate longer videos, each new chunk must attend to previously generated
chunks for temporal coherence. A naive KV cache that stores keys and values for all pre-
viously generated chunks would grow linearly with video length. We instead use a rolling
cache of fixed size, designed around two roles:

    1. the first chunk, kept as a fixed anchor on which subsequent chunks can condition.
       This mirrors the attention sink mechanism used to stabilize streaming language mod-
       els [48] and also adopted for streaming long-video generation [29];
    2. the last n chunks (n = 1 in our setting), which provide the immediate temporal context
       for local motion continuity. We keep a single past chunk (n = 1): enlarging the win-
       dow to n = 2 yields no quality improvement while increasing cache size and lowering
       throughput (supplementary material, Tab. 9).

All other chunks are evicted from the cache once they fall outside this window, which yields
constant inference memory regardless of video duration. Moreover, thanks to the per-chunk
mixed-timestep training described in Section 3.2, cached entries only need to store keys and
values at the lowest noise level reached at the last denoising step of the chunk, rather than at
every diffusion step. The same attention pattern is reproduced at training time by the block-
causal mask (Figure 2a), in a single forward pass and without maintaining a KV cache.
COHEN ET AL.: AURA: AUDIO-DRIVEN STREAMING AVATAR                                            9

RoPE indexing. At inference, generated-frame positions grow without bound, while the
reference frame is anchored at a fixed temporal RoPE position 10 to 40 latents ahead of the
current generation front at training time (Section 3.1 and supplementary material A.1). To
preserve this training-time configuration, we re-index the reference every 30 new generated
latent frames, shifting it by +30 each time so that its offset relative to the generation front
cycles between +40 and +10 (Figure 7 in supplementary material). This keeps the relative
position of the reference within the regime the model was trained on, and avoids the position
drift that would otherwise accumulate over long autoregressive rollouts.

3.6   Synthetic Talking-Head Dataset
We find that existing audio-driven video datasets are ill-suited to our training setup: the
TalkVerse training set is released only as YouTube URLs and is difficult to obtain reliably,
while the Hallo3 dataset [9] contains clips too short to match our teacher’s single-chunk
generation window of ∼ 5 s. We instead build a synthetic dataset by running the TalkVerse
teacher on conditioning inputs we control, and release it to support Stage 1 ODE teacher
forcing (Sec. 3.3) and future work on audio-driven distillation.
Conditioning inputs. We draw reference images and their associated text prompts from
Hallo3 dataset [9], and pair each (image, prompt) to a synthetic speech track: We first obtain
textual English audio transcripts of Youtube videos provided in the LiveCC dataset [7]. Then,
we generate speech from a subset of these transcript using an off-the-shelf text-to-speech
model [54]. The resulting clips average 4.8 s, matching the teacher’s chunk size.
Video generation. For each (image, prompt, audio) triplet, we run the TalkVerse teacher
at 720p and 25 Hz with 50 ODE steps and a guidance scale of 4.5. Reference image quality
is heterogeneous across Hallo3, which also leads to diverse quality in the resulting video
clips; No quality filtering is applied. For each generated video, we also store the teacher’s
latent ODE trajectory at five timesteps: the initial noise (t = 1), three intermediate states
(t ∈ {0.75, 0.5, 0.25}), and the final clean latent (t = 0). Latents follow the TalkVerse VAE
compression (4×16×16 over time and space). We additionally store the decoded clean
videos as MP4 files alongside the original audio tracks.
Release. The final dataset totals 30,000 clips (38.7 h of synthetic videos, compared to ∼ 70 h
for Hallo3 and CelebV). We release the audio tracks, decoded videos, and the five-timestep
latent ODE trajectories to foster reproducibility and future work on talking heads generation.


4     Experiments
4.1   Experimental Setup
Architecture and initialization. The student is initialized from TalkVerse (5B parameters)
and trained with LoRA adapters [21] of rank 128 on the attention layers. We additionally
unfreeze the timestep embedding and the output head of the student, since our model uses
the per-chunk mixed-timestep scheme of Section 3.2 rather than the single shared timestep
of the teacher. Unless stated otherwise, our model generates videos at 720p in chunks of
nc = 4 latent frames with 4 diffusion denoising steps per chunk. The output video plays back
at 25 Hz, the native output rate of TalkVerse; we use fps elsewhere to denote throughput.
Two-stage LoRA training. Stage 1 (ODE teacher forcing) runs for 120K steps (108 h) at
learning rate 1e-5, batch size 256. We then merge the Stage 1 LoRA into the base weights and
10                            COHEN ET AL.: AURA: AUDIO-DRIVEN STREAMING AVATAR

re-initialize a fresh LoRA of the same rank for Stage 2 (DMD with backward simulation),
avoiding compounded adapters. Stage 2 runs for 5K steps (30 h) at learning rate 3.5e-7, batch
size 256, with 5 critic updates per student update. The critic is initialized from the teacher
and, operating on its single shared timestep, has only its LoRA adapters trained. Remaining
hyperparameters are in the supplementary material (A.3); all training uses 64 H100 GPUs.
Baselines. We compare AURA against four open-source audio-driven avatar generation
models. TalkVerse [47] , our teacher, serves as a high-quality reference for the distillation.
Wan-S2V 14B [16] and Hallo3 [9] are two recent large-scale non-causal S2V model. Live
Avatar [23] is a streaming few-step approach and the closest competitor to AURA in infer-
ence regime. For all baselines we use the authors’ released code and checkpoints, and run
inference under each method’s recommended settings.
Benchmarks. We evaluate on two test sets we generate. H ALLO 3 TEST consists of 100
first frames and prompts randomly sampled from the Hallo3 test set [9], each re-paired with
a real English speech track from an internal dataset to assess speaker generalization; all clips
are 10 s. C ELEB V-HQ TEST consists of 150 first frames from CelebV-HQ [57], each paired
with 8–20 s of TTS-synthesized speech (same pipeline as Sec. 3.6) and the generic prompt
“a person is talking”; we synthesize rather than reuse audio because CelebV-HQ clips are
too short (∼5 s) to test longer sequences.
Metrics. We evaluate along five axes. Visual quality: FID [18], FVD [45] on 16-frame
windows, reported at three temporal offsets, @0 (initialization), @101 and @109 (windows
centered on and starting at a mid-video chunk boundary, capturing inter-chunk discontinu-
ities), and JEDi [32] at the same offsets, an MMD-based distance on V-JEPA features more
reliable at our limited sample counts. Temporal consistency: the warping error (WE), the
mean L1 residual after RAFT [44] optical-flow alignment of consecutive frames, captur-
ing flicker and chunk-boundary artifacts. Audio–visual alignment: Sync-C and Sync-D [8]
(SyncNet). Identity preservation: CSIM, the mean cosine similarity between ArcFace [13]
embeddings of the reference and generated frames. Inference timing: on a single H100 GPU,
generating one clip from a fixed 10 s English audio and a 960×960 reference at each model’s
native resolution and frame rate; we report throughput (fps) for the DiT and VAE decoder
separately and the end-to-end Total (excluding loading and warm-up). Total is not a simple
composition of the two: for Live Avatar, offloading the DiT or VAE between calls to fit in
GPU memory adds overhead.

4.3    Ablations
We ablate the main design choices of our method. Unless stated otherwise, all ablations use
the 5B student trained on the full pipeline and are evaluated on the H ALLO 3 TEST set with
6.3 s clips, shorter than the 10 s clips used in the main results (Sec. 4.2) to reduce compute.
Number of diffusion steps. Table 3 reports quality metrics when generating each chunk in
{1, 2, 3, 4} diffusion steps. Per-frame and per-window quality (FID, FVD, JEDi) degrades
gradually from 4 to 2 steps and drops sharply at 1 step. Identity preservation (CSIM) follows
the same trend. Lip sync quality (Sync-C, Sync-D) remains essentially constant across all
step counts. Temporal consistency metric (Warp Error) reaches lower values as the number
of steps decreases, but this reflects a known failure mode of these metrics rather than a real
improvement: with fewer steps, generated frames become blurrier and lose high-frequency
content, which leaves fewer details for the metrics to flag as inconsistent across frames. Qual-
itatively, videos become progressively blurrier as the number of steps decreases (Sec. 4.4).
We adopt 4 steps in all other experiments as the best trade-off between per-frame quality and
inference speed.

the DMD stage substantially improves all perceptual quality metrics (FID, FVD, JEDi) and

to the teacher in the few-step regime. Lip-sync metrics also improve. As in the previous
ablation, the temporal consistency metric (Warp) reaches lower value with Stage 1 only, but
this reflects the same metric artifact on blurrier outputs rather than a real improvement.

5     Conclusions
We presented AURA, a 5B causal few-step speech-to-video model for streaming talking-
head generation. Distilling a bidirectional teacher into a same-size 4-step causal student
through a two-stage pipeline and a rolling KV cache, AURA matches its TalkVerse teacher
on FVD and JEDi at a 17× speedup, delivering 720p video at 9.7 fps on a single GPU.
We also release a 30,000-clip synthetic dataset with teacher ODE trajectories to support
future work on audio-driven distillation. Two limitations remain. The rolling KV cache
preserves global identity, yet slight discontinuities can still appear at chunk boundaries on
long sequences; and at 4 steps AURA remains short of the 25 Hz native rate required for true
real-time generation on a single GPU. Distilling to a smaller backbone or further reducing the
number of denoising steps is a natural path toward real-time, fluid streaming for interactive
avatars.

A     Supplementary Material
A.1    TalkVerse
We use TalkVerse [47] as our teacher model, a 5B-parameter audio-driven video genera-
tion model built on the Wan2.2-5B [46] Diffusion Transformer (DiT) backbone, originally
pretrained for text-and-image-to-video generation. TalkVerse extends the Wan-S2V [16]
design for speech-conditioned video synthesis to the Wan2.2 backbone, and trades the 14B-
parameter scale of Wan-S2V for a smaller 5B model, trained on a high-resolution open-
source dataset.
Latent space. TalkVerse uses the Wan2.2 video VAE, which downsamples by (t, h, w) =
(4, 16, 16), a higher compression ratio than the (4, 8, 8) commonly used in prior video dif-
fusion models (e.g. Wan2.1). This yields a 4× reduction in latent token count at the same
resolution, enabling efficient high-resolution (720p) generation.
Conditioning. The model is conditioned on three inputs: (i) a reference identity image,
(ii) a speech audio track, and (iii) a text prompt describing the video content. Audio is
encoded with Wav2Vec2 [1] and injected into a subset of DiT blocks via cross-attention
from visual tokens to audio tokens, with each latent frame attending to a temporally aligned
audio segment. The text prompt is encoded with T5-XXL [35] and injected through cross-
attention. TalkVerse is trained on the large-scale TalkVerse-2.3M dataset of high-resolution
audio-synchronized clips.
Long-video generation: temporal context. To generate minute-long videos, TalkVerse
uses a sliding-window mechanism: each window denoises a fixed number of frames condi-
tioned on a window of preceding frames, referred to as motion frames. To keep the token
budget tractable, the motion frames are compressed by a FramePack module [55] and as-
signed negative positional embeddings to mark them as past context.
Long-video generation: reference frame placement. The reference identity image is as-
signed a positional embedding placed after the denoised video window, specifically position
40 for a denoised window of 30 latent frames. This forward offset makes the model “chase”
the reference rather than copy it, which the authors report avoids copy-paste artifacts and
looped body movements when the same reference is reused across sliding windows.
Training and inference. TalkVerse is trained with LoRA on the DiT attention layers, while
audio projection, audio cross-attention, and FramePack-related modules are fully fine-tuned.
Inference uses 50 denoising steps with a flow-matching solver and classifier-free guidance.


A.2    Sampling scheme
Within each chunk, we adopt the predict-and-renoise scheme of DMD2 [51]. Standard flow
matching integration zt−∆t = zt + vθ (zt ,t, c) · ∆t assumes that intermediate states lie on the
learned ODE trajectory, an assumption that breaks down in the few-step regime, where each
step spans a large interval of noise levels. Given a schedule of K = 4 denoising steps with
timesteps 1 = t0 > t1 > · · · > tK = 0, the chunk is initialized as zt0 ∼ N (0, I), and each step
k proceeds in two parts. The student first predicts the velocity vθ (ztk ,tk , c) and reconstructs
an estimate of the clean latent under the linear flow matching parameterization:
                                   (k)
                                  x̂0 = ztk − tk · vθ (ztk ,tk , c).                          (4)
22                              COHEN ET AL.: AURA: AUDIO-DRIVEN STREAMING AVATAR

This estimate is then re-noised to the next noise level tk+1 with fresh Gaussian noise ε ∼
N (0, I):
                                                      (k)
                              ztk+1 = (1 − tk+1 ) · x̂0 + tk+1 · ε.                     (5)
                                          (K−1)
The final latent of the chunk is x̂0 = x̂0 , obtained at the last denoising step. The re-noising
in Eq. 5 projects each intermediate estimate back onto the manifold of noisy latents the stu-
dent was trained on, rather than following a straight-line integration of the velocity field. The
same scheme is used during Stage 2 training to roll out the student without backpropagation
and produce the noisy states {x̂tk } used by backward simulation (Sec. 3.4), keeping training
and inference aligned.

A.3     Training Details
We implement the chunk-wise causal attention mask (sliding window plus initial-chunk sink,
Fig. 2) with FlexAttention [15], which compiles custom attention-mask patterns efficiently.
This is only required at training, where attention spans the full sequence; at inference the
rolling KV cache reduces attention to the cached chunks, so no custom masking is needed.

A.8      Rolling cache window size
We ablate the number of past chunks n retained in the rolling KV cache (Sec. 3.5). Our
default keeps a single past chunk (n = 1) alongside the first-chunk anchor; here we compare
it against a larger window that also retains the second-to-last chunk (n = 2). Both variants
use the same 4-step student and are evaluated on the H ALLO 3 validation subset (6.3 s clips).
As reported in Table 9, enlarging the window to n = 2 improves no metric, while increasing
cache size and lowering throughput. This supports our minimal single-chunk cache as the
best quality–efficiency trade-off.
